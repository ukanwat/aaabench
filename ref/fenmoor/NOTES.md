# fenmoor — reference board

Anchor: the New Jersey Meadowlands, (40.8000, −74.0800), plus the Kearny reach of the same marsh at (40.7710, −74.1060) and Kearny Point / the Hackensack at (40.7500, −74.0900)

**Anchor and coverage warning — this is the thinnest board of the nine, and here is exactly why.**
- MAP_PLAN's coordinate (40.8000, −74.0800) sits in the middle of the Meadowlands, and Wikimedia geosearch there returns **almost nothing but photographs of the New Jersey Turnpike taken from the New Jersey Turnpike** — twelve of fifteen results were highway-sign documentation. Geosearching the nature reserve at DeKorte Park (40.7880, −74.0940) returns **bird portraits** — pintail, yellowlegs, sandpiper, tern — and nothing showing the landscape.
- Mapillary coverage of the marsh exists but is **entirely from the Turnpike at 100 km/h**, so every street-level frame is a motorway shot with the reeds in the middle distance. There is no coverage on any of the marsh access roads, the landfill roads or the DeKorte boardwalk. KartaView returns `n = 0`.
- The consequence: four of the ten images here are keyword-sourced from other places (a California baylands pylon line, a European reedbed, a Virginia landfill, a British flooded quarry and allotment) and are **labelled as such**. Everything about the actual marsh — reed height, water colour, the way the roads sit on it — is taken from the three Turnpike frames, which are honest but limited.
- **If Fenmoor is going to be more than Tier 3, this board needs a second pass with a better anchor.** A reedbed–landfill–pylon district with public access at ground level would be better anchored on somewhere like Rainham Marshes or the Wallasea/Thames estuary landfill edge, where Geograph coverage is dense and at eye height.

## What is actually there — observations

- **Ground:** There are only three ground surfaces here and no pavement anywhere. (1) **Road on embankment** — asphalt with a white edge line, a galvanised W-beam guardrail on rusted posts a metre off the edge, and a narrow gravel shoulder that drops straight into reeds. (2) **Water** — shallow, still, and its colour is a **dark olive-brown, almost black in shadow**, with the reeds reflected in it as vertical smears. (3) **Bare graded fill** — the landfill and quarry roads are dark compacted soil and crushed hardcore, rutted, with standing water in the ruts and litter blown into the edges. The reed goes right to the edge of every hard surface with no mown margin and no transition.
- **Facades and ground floor:** Effectively none. What buildings exist are objects, not frontages: a small white operator's cabin on a bridge, a portacabin, a shed. The one place with a "facade" vocabulary is the allotment, where every structure is **improvised** — a greenhouse of mismatched glass, a shed of reused boards, a woven hurdle, a green mesh fence, a water butt.
- **Roofline and clutter:** The skyline is made of four things and nothing else: **lattice transmission towers**, a **flat-topped capped landfill mound**, a **distant lattice bridge**, and the far city towers in haze. That is the entire vertical composition of the district, and the towers are by far the most numerous.
- **Verticality — poles, cables, signs:** The pylons are everything. From the road they read as **small grey lattice shapes evenly spaced along the whole horizon** — a dotted line, not a few heroic objects. Close up they are waisted lattice with three or four crossarm levels, and the runs contain **more than one tower design**, so the profile changes along the line. The conductors sag deeply — the mid-span dip is a big fraction of the tower height — and they cross the road diagonally. Add: aircraft warning spheres, and red obstruction lights at night at two heights. Ground-level verticals are guardrail posts, yellow marker posts on the tip roads, and the odd steel mesh litter fence.
- **Materials and their ageing:** Galvanised steel going chalk-grey with rust at every bolt and cut end. Timber piles rotted to silver-grey points and leaning. Riveted black-painted steel on the bridge, with the paint failing to orange along every rivet line and horizontal ledge. Concrete counterweight blocks stained and streaked. Portable mesh fence panels bent and patched with cable ties. Plastic film caught in every fence and every bush at the tip.
- **Colour:** The reedbed is the district. In late summer and autumn it is a **warm straw-gold with a purple-brown haze at the top where the plumes are**; through winter it bleaches to a **pale fawn-grey** and stays standing; in spring it is dead fawn with green shoots at the base. Against that: **olive-brown-black water**, **chalky grey galvanised steel**, the **mown green cap of the landfill mound**, **rust-orange dead bracken and sumac**, and enormous **blue-and-white cumulus skies** — the sky is more than half of every frame here. At night: dirty orange sky-glow and red points.
- **Light:** Completely unobstructed. Nothing casts a shadow onto anything else, so the light is whatever the sky is doing, in full. This is the district for weather: towering cumulus with hard shadows sweeping across the reeds, flat pewter overcast, and low raking sun that lights the reed plumes from behind and makes the whole bed glow while the water goes black. **There is no street lighting at all**, so at night the only light is vehicle headlights, distant sky-glow, and the red lights on the pylons.
- **Vegetation:** One species dominates absolutely — **common reed, *Phragmites australis***, in a monoculture that runs to the horizon, 2.5–4 m tall, canes dead-straight and vertical, leaves hanging at 45°, a drooping one-sided plume at every tip. Everything else is an edge species on the fill: **staghorn sumac, tree of heaven, grey birch, common reed's own dead thatch, mugwort, goldenrod, and rust-orange dead bracken**. Scattered dark evergreen scrub in clumps on the drier mounds. On the capped landfill: mown coarse grass and nothing else, because the cap is only a metre deep. In the allotments: everything, in rows, cultivated.
- **People and vehicles:** Nobody on foot anywhere in the anchor imagery. The vehicles are through-traffic — cars, and a lot of **articulated lorries** — plus the working plant on the tip: **yellow tracked dozers and compactors**. The living population of this district is birds: gulls in a swirling cloud over the tip face and standing on the haul road, plus waders, terns and swallows in the marsh.
- **The tells:**
  - Reeds growing right up to the guardrail with no mown verge.
  - A guardrail whose posts are rusted while the beam is still bright.
  - Two different pylon designs in the same line.
  - A flat-topped mound with a mown green top and nothing on it.
  - Plastic film caught in every fence at the tip.
  - A portable litter fence of mesh panels along the crest of the tip.
  - Hundreds of gulls standing on a haul road as well as flying.
  - Rotted timber pile fenders leaning at every angle in the water.
  - Paint failing along every rivet line on the black bridge steel.
  - A galvanised water butt with a brass tap on an allotment.
  - Red obstruction lights at two heights, hanging in orange murk, with nothing else lit.
  - Dead reed standing all winter — it does not fall down.

**Where the photographs contradicted what I would have assumed:**
1. **The reed does not die back and collapse.** It stands all winter as a pale fawn wall of straight canes with the plumes still on. A winter Fenmoor is not a flat brown mud plain — it is the same 3 m wall in a different colour.
2. The pylons read as **many small shapes across the whole horizon**, not a few big ones. And a single line contains more than one tower design.
3. **The landfill's active face is defined by the birds** — a dense moving cloud of gulls, and hundreds more standing on the ground. That, plus the litter fence, is the whole read.
4. There is **no street lighting**. At night the district is black with a red constellation floating in it.
5. The flooded quarry has **no shore** — the rock face goes straight into the water with an overhanging turf lip above.

## Numbers I could measure or infer
Estimates. The Turnpike frames give good scale off the lorries (trailer 13.6 m long, 4.1 m high); the rest is scaled off plant and people.

- **Phragmites:** **2.5–4.0 m tall**, canes 8–12 mm, at maybe 60–120 stems/m²; plume 200–350 mm long and drooping. The bed is a **visual wall from about 15 m away** — you cannot see over or into it from the ground.
- **Road on embankment:** 3 or 4 lanes at 3.6 m plus 2.5–3.0 m shoulders; embankment top **1.5–3.0 m above the marsh**; guardrail 0.75 m high, posts at 1.9 m, set 0.6–1.0 m off the edge line.
- **Transmission tower:** **35–55 m** for a double-circuit lattice; base spread 8–12 m; **span 250–350 m**; mid-span conductor sag 8–14 m; lowest conductor 12–18 m above ground. A line of ten towers occupies about 3 km — i.e. the whole width of a district.
- **Capped landfill mound:** 15–30 m above the marsh, side slopes about 1:3–1:4 (18–20°), **flat mown top 100–300 m across**, with gas well heads and a flare stack somewhere on it.
- **Landfill active face:** slope 1:2–1:3, face height 4–8 m, litter fence panels 3.5 m tall × 3.0 m wide on skids; haul road 8–10 m wide.
- **Flooded quarry:** rock face **8–20 m** from turf lip to water, near-vertical, with a 0.3–0.6 m overhanging turf lip; water surface flat to the toe with no beach.
- **Allotment plot:** roughly **10 × 25 m** (a quarter of a UK "ten rod" plot is 5 × 10 m; full plots run 250 m²); greenhouse 2.4 × 3.6 m, ridge at 2.4 m; water butt 0.6 m dia × 1.0 m; paths 0.6 m.
- **Bascule bridge:** fixed truss span 40–55 m, truss depth 5–6 m; counterweight frame rising 12–15 m above the deck; operator's cabin 3 × 4 m; timber fender piles 300–400 mm dia at 1.0–1.5 m centres, standing 1.5–2.5 m above water.
- **Sky:** in every frame here the horizon sits at **15–25 % of the image height**. That ratio is the district.
