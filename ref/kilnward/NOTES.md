# kilnward — reference board

Anchors: Olneyville / Valley St, Providence RI, (41.8180, −71.4380) · Lowell, MA (Merrimack & Pawtucket canals), (42.6460, −71.3120)

Note on the second anchor: (42.6460, −71.3120) is **Lowell**, not Lawrence — and that is lucky, because Lowell is the one American mill city built *around a canal system*, which is exactly what MAP_PLAN's "dead canal" needs. Mapillary coverage in Lowell is thin (five usable images in a 1 km box) but the geosearch coverage is good. Providence/Olneyville has dense 2018–2025 dashcam coverage, and it happens to sit right under the Route 6/10 viaduct, which is the direct analogue of MAP_PLAN's Ring.

Because Ashmouth's kilns are **bottle kilns** and no American mill district has those, three images come from Stoke-on-Trent by keyword. They are labelled as such.

## What is actually there — observations

- **Ground:** Asphalt with a **double yellow centre line** on every through street, and that yellow is the district's brightest colour at ground level. The surface is patched in long ribbons along the joints and around every utility cover, and near the viaduct it carries winter salt whitening in the gutters. Canal-side surfaces are different: mown grass, a 1.8 m tarmac or bound-gravel path, granite coping. Mill yards are cracked asphalt with the parking bays painted straight onto it, right up against the building with no kerb, and puddles that never drain. Under the viaduct the ground goes to loose gravel and concrete channel.
- **Facades and ground floor:** Red brick, laid in common bond, four storeys, with **segmental-arched window heads** and a stone or brick sill band. Windows are large — much larger than a residential building of the same date — and are steel-framed with many small panes, painted black or dark green. The later mills are concrete frame, painted cream or buff, with the same huge windows in a flatter grid. At ground level there is no shopfront: there is a row of identical **black doors** with a number over each, red standpipe inlets, a loading bay with a roller shutter, and a screwed-on tenant directory. Where a building is dead, the windows are boarded with plywood cut per opening.
- **Roofline and clutter:** Flat or very shallow, behind a corbelled brick cornice. On the ridge, a **clerestory monitor** — a raised glazed strip running the length of the building — which is the mill's identifying roof feature. Above that: a **cylindrical water tank on a steel frame**, one round tapering brick chimney per mill complex (far taller than the building, with a corbelled flared cap and cast-iron ladder rungs up one side), a stair tower with a pitched cap, and modern packaged AC plant scattered on the flat. Aerials and dishes appear only where the mill has become flats.
- **Verticality — poles, cables, signs:** Timber poles carrying overhead wires *diagonally across the street corners*, so the sky over a mill junction is criss-crossed. Two generations of street lighting stand side by side and clash: a black cast-iron "heritage" column with a bell lantern, and a plain grey steel column with a cobra head, sometimes 30 m apart, sometimes 2 m apart. **Billboards on steel poles standing above the viaduct deck** — sited for the motorway, ignoring the street. Chain link everywhere along the road/rail/canal edges, black-painted where it is municipal and galvanised where it is a contractor's.
- **Materials and their ageing:** Brick that runs orange-red to dark plum, with lime mortar eroded and the odd wholesale cement repointing patch. Granite in the canal walls, buff-grey, with a rough rubble course at the waterline where the algae line sits and dressed ashlar above. Concrete of the 1960s viaduct **spalling badly with orange reinforcement showing** and dark water streaks under every deck joint. Plywood boarding going silver-grey and delaminating at the bottom edge. Painted steel window frames failing first at the sill. The kiln brick is **sooted to near black on the outside** with the original buff showing only where it has been repaired.
- **Colour:** Red-plum brick; grey-buff granite; the black-green of the steel windows; bottle-green railings; **oxblood red** on the substation bay; the strong yellow of road paint; lime green on a billboard; silver-and-white graffiti; and a small amount of neon red on a rooftop sign. The canal water is **near-black at dusk and olive-green in daylight**, and it does not read as blue at any time. Winter here is grey-brown: bare elms, salt on the road, plum brick under flat cloud.
- **Light:** 41.8 °N. The buildings are only four to five storeys so the streets get direct sun for a decent part of the day — but the **viaduct casts a permanent hard-edged band of shadow** across the district that moves but never leaves, and the underpass beneath it is dark at noon. The canal cut is shaded by mature trees on both banks and stays dim; at dusk it goes to pure black water with one warm reflection in it. Night lighting: cobra heads on the arterial, heritage lanterns on the canal walk, and **long yellow fluorescent battens on the underpass soffit** — three different colour temperatures within one block.
- **Vegetation:** Two kinds. Municipal: mown grass along the canal, mature Norway maple, horse chestnut and elm arching over the water, ornamental grasses in a mill's landscaped car park. Volunteer: buddleia, rosebay willowherb, sumac and birch seedlings in the canal coping, in the mill yard cracks, and all over the embankment under the viaduct. Winter strips the first out entirely and the second is bare brown sticks.
- **People and vehicles:** Very few people. Vehicles are the giveaway: **pickup trucks and box trucks**, not saloons — a food-service semi-trailer occupying the whole nearside lane, contractors' pickups in the mill car park, an orange tracked excavator parked on the viaduct deck. Traffic on the viaduct is fast and continuous and completely disconnected from the street below it.
- **The tells:**
  - Exposed rebar and rust streaks on the viaduct piers.
  - A billboard standing above the viaduct so it can only be read at 60 mph.
  - A cast-iron heritage lamppost bolted down beside a jersey barrier.
  - A galvanised camera monopole planted 2 m from a heritage lamp on the canal walk.
  - Plywood boarding cut per window, each panel a different shade of weathered grey.
  - A window AC unit through the brick of a listed mill.
  - Fire standpipe inlets, painted red, at 1 m above the pavement.
  - The tenant directory screwed to the brick next to a Victorian door.
  - Graffiti covering an underpass wall to exactly 3 m and no higher.
  - Fly-tipped doors and cupboards at the base of the kiln.
  - Buddleia growing out of the canal coping stones.
  - The canal fenced off from the street it runs beside.

**Where the photographs contradicted what I would have assumed:**
1. **Bottle kilns are black, fat and hemmed in.** I would have drawn tall slender red-brick cones standing in open ground. They are squat, soot-black, iron-banded, and packed so tightly between the works buildings that you only see the top two-thirds. Four of them "preserved" would read as four dark lumps in a brick mass, not as four towers.
2. The mill chimney is **round**, tapering, with a corbelled flared cap and iron rungs — not square. The square brick shaft in these photos is a smaller flue on the works building, not the main stack.
3. The most important roof feature is the **clerestory monitor along the ridge**, not a sawtooth. I searched hard for sawtooth north-light roofs at both anchors and found essentially none at street level — the sawtooth is a single-storey weaving-shed form and these are multi-storey spinning mills. **If MAP_PLAN wants sawtooth roofs in Kilnward, they belong on the low single-storey sheds, and the multi-storey mills should get a monitor instead.**
4. The canal is not a picturesque waterway — it is **fenced off from the street on both sides**, with the water 2–3 m below the coping, and it reads as a slot, not a feature.

## Numbers I could measure or infer
Estimates, scaled off cars, doors, people and the ISO-standard fencing.

- **Mill:** 4–5 storeys, floor to floor **3.6–4.2 m** (taller than housing, shorter than a warehouse); parapet at 16–19 m; block **18–22 m deep** and 60–120 m long; window bays at 3.0–3.6 m centres; window opening 1.5–1.8 m wide × 2.4–2.8 m high.
- **Round chimney:** 3.5–4.5 m base diameter tapering to ~2.0 m, height **35–45 m** — roughly 2.5× the mill parapet, and the tallest thing in the district.
- **Rooftop water tank:** 3.5–4.5 m diameter × 4–5 m tall, on a steel frame lifting it 3–4 m above the roof.
- **Clerestory monitor:** 2.5–3.0 m wide, raised 1.5–2.0 m above the main roof, running the full length.
- **Bottle kiln (hovel):** base diameter **7–9 m**, height **12–16 m** to the top of the neck — about 1.8× as tall as it is wide. Iron hoops at roughly 1.5 m vertical spacing. The wad hole doors around the base are ~1.6 m tall.
- **Canal:** water **8–11 m wide**, walls 2.5–3.5 m from coping to water; granite blocks 0.5 m high × 1.0–1.5 m long; railing 1.1 m high with 100 mm bar spacing; towpath/walk 1.8–2.5 m.
- **Viaduct:** deck soffit **4.6–5.2 m** over the road at the underpass; piers 1.2 m square at 12–18 m spacing; deck carrying 4 lanes at 3.6 m plus shoulders ≈ 20 m wide.
- **Street:** 11–14 m kerb to kerb, two lanes plus kerbside parking; footway 1.8–2.5 m, and against a mill wall often none at all.
- **Chain link:** 1.8 m along the canal, 2.4 m plus barbed arms at the rail; posts at 3.0 m.
- **Utility poles:** 10–11 m, 35–45 m spacing, wires crossing the junction diagonally at 6–7 m.
- **Graffiti band on the underpass wall:** 0 to ~3.0 m, i.e. the reach of a person standing on the ground with an arm up. Nothing above it.
