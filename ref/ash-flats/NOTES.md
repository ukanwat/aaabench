# ash-flats — reference board

Anchors as given in MAP_PLAN: (39.2664, −76.5836) · (40.6743, −74.0146)

**Anchor problem — read this first.** The first coordinate, (39.2664, −76.5836), is **Fort McHenry National Monument**, not a container port. Wikimedia geosearch at that point returns cannons, ramparts and the visitor centre; the Mapillary imagery there is a park access road. The Baltimore container terminals are 3–5 km east-south-east: **Seagirt Marine Terminal ≈ (39.2540, −76.5480)** and **Dundalk Marine Terminal ≈ (39.2480, −76.5310)**, with the port arterial (Broening Highway / Keith Avenue) around **(39.2590, −76.5379)**. I gathered from the real terminals and their access roads instead, and I recommend MAP_PLAN's anchor be corrected to one of those. The second anchor, Red Hook, Brooklyn, is correct and excellent — it gives both the working container terminal and the 19th-century brick warehouse street that Ash Flats also needs.

Anchors actually used: Broening Hwy / Seagirt & Dundalk gates, Baltimore, (39.2590, −76.5379) · Van Brunt St & Red Hook Container Terminal, Brooklyn, (40.6733, −74.0183)

## What is actually there — observations

- **Ground:** Two completely different surfaces, and which one you get tells you what the land is for. **Port roads and terminal yards are unpainted portland-cement concrete** — pale grey-buff, laid in ~4.5 m bays with transverse and longitudinal saw joints filled with black sealant, stained in dark tyre-track ribbons and pale rectangular patches. **Warehouse streets are asphalt in its worst condition** — potholed, patched with at least four different mixes, cracked in a map pattern, with the old rail still in it. Rails are let in flush with a groove either side and they are *shiny on the head and rust everywhere else*, and they cross the road at an angle. Kerbs are concrete, often broken away; large stretches have no kerb at all, just a lip where the concrete stops and the gravel starts. Lane paint on the arterials is thermoplastic white, thick, and worn to a ghost in the wheel tracks; the centre line is a **double yellow** and it is the only colour on the ground for hundreds of metres.
- **Facades and ground floor:** There is no ground floor in the retail sense. The 19th-century warehouse elevation is: rough hand-made brick, a plinth of granite or bluestone, round-arched openings at 4.5–5.5 m centres, each with **paired black iron shutters** or a black timber door, a painted circle with the bay number, and cast-iron star or S anchor plates on a grid. Later industrial is a steel portal frame with profiled metal cladding, a rusty red or faded blue roof, one roller shutter and a personnel door beside it. The one thing every building has at ground level is a **bollard** — yellow steel pipes filled with concrete, in threes and fours, protecting every corner and every door.
- **Roofline and clutter:** Flat or shallow-pitch. On the brick warehouses: parapet, then a scatter of grey packaged AC units, a brick lift-overrun or water-tank tower, and a vertical steel ladder with a cage. On the sheds: ridge vents, a run of translucent rooflight panels, and rust bleeding down from every fixing. Above all of it: **floodlight masts, 20–30 m, with clusters of six to twelve heads** — they are the tallest thing in the district apart from the cranes, and they are what make it look like a port from a distance at night.
- **Verticality — poles, cables, signs:** Timber utility poles, tall (10–12 m), carrying six to eight conductors plus a messenger, in a long straight file along one side of the arterial, with the cobra streetlight bracketed off the same pole. Over the terminal roads there are **steel lattice sign gantries** with big green guide signs and the traffic signals hung from them on short vertical drops. Chain link everywhere, 2.4 m plus three strands of barbed or razor wire on outward-leaning arms, with dark green or dark blue privacy slats where anyone cares. Yellow-and-black striped guard posts. Every gate has a portacabin beside it.
- **Materials and their ageing:** Brick that has gone orange-pink where washed and near-black where sheltered, with the mortar eroded back 5–10 mm on the exposed faces. Iron shutters painted black over black over rust, and at least one pair in every run has failed to a deep orange-red. Galvanised steel gone chalky white-grey. Concrete stained with rust runs from every embedded fixing, and with black rubber where wheels turn. Corrugated metal fences dented and graffitied to hip height and clean above it.
- **Colour:** Grey-buff concrete; asphalt in three greys plus black tar; orange-pink brick with black soot; **safety orange** (crane, barrels, cones) and **safety yellow** (bollards, guard posts, road centre line); the muted saturated slabs of stacked containers — rust red, maersk-ish light blue, dark green, oxide brown, white; galvanised chalk-grey; rust-red shed roofs. The sky is a much larger share of the frame here than anywhere else in the city, and in the overcast images it is a flat pale grey-green.
- **Light:** Flat and unshaded. There is nothing tall enough to cast a shadow across the road, so the whole district is lit by the full hemisphere of sky and the shadows are short and hard on sunny days and effectively absent when overcast. At the water edge, the light comes off the water too and lifts the underside of everything. At night: sparse cobra heads on the arterial giving pools 30–40 m apart with real darkness between them, plus **the floodlight masts in the terminal, which are far brighter than the street and throw the cranes into silhouette with a bright ground under them**. That contrast — a black empty road looking into a blazing yard — is the district's night image.
- **Vegetation:** Nobody planted anything. What grows is **tree of heaven (Ailanthus), sumac, mugwort, ragweed and knotweed**, in the strip between fence and kerb, in the cracks of the concrete, and against the base of every wall. The exception is the mown grass verge on the newer port roads, which is municipal and closely cut with young honey locusts at 15 m centres, and looks wrong beside everything else. Around the tanks the ground is bare graded gravel with nothing on it at all.
- **People and vehicles:** No pedestrians. In eight street-level frames I count two people, both walking past the warehouses. Vehicles: a transit bus, a handful of private cars, an orange-and-white barrel truck, one crawler crane, and enormous numbers of **parked non-freight vehicles** — school buses, imported cars in rows, and a chassis park of hundreds of empty trailer frames. Freight itself appears mostly as *stored* boxes, not moving ones.
- **The tells:**
  - The rail is still in the road and it is not straight, not square, and not connected to anything any more.
  - Painted bay numbers in circles on the brick, half worn off.
  - One pair of iron shutters rusted red in a run of thirty black ones.
  - Yellow bollards in threes at every corner.
  - A three-strand barbed-wire arm leaning outward, sagging between posts.
  - Weeds growing through a piece of dead rolling stock.
  - Graffiti to exactly hip-to-shoulder height on the corrugated fence and nowhere above it.
  - Tar snakes and four generations of asphalt patch in one 20 m stretch.
  - A stencilled number and a red-painted vent on a white tank.
  - The chassis park — hundreds of empty trailer frames nose-to-tail.
  - Floodlight masts standing over an empty yard.
  - A gatehouse portacabin with an air conditioner in the window.

**Where the photographs contradicted what I would have assumed:**
1. The port roads are **concrete, pale, and jointed** — not black asphalt. That changes the whole value of the ground plane; the district reads *light* underfoot, not dark, and the containers and the rust sit on a bright surface.
2. Container cranes park with their **booms raised to near vertical**, so the terminal silhouette is a row of tall inverted-L shapes pointing at the sky, not a row of horizontal jibs over the water.
3. Most of the land is **storing something that isn't freight** — school buses, imported cars, empty chassis. That is a better answer to "how do I fill a half-empty port" than more containers.
4. The waterline is riprap and a handrail almost everywhere. Only the actual berths have a quay wall.
5. Tanks are white with a company logo painted large, not bare metal.

## Numbers I could measure or infer
Estimates, scaled off ISO containers (12.19 m long, 2.44 m wide, 2.59 m high — the most useful ruler on this board), buses (12 m), poles and doors.

- **Terminal road / arterial:** 4 lanes at 3.6 m plus shoulders = **16–20 m kerb to kerb**, often with a 3–6 m grass or gravel verge each side and no footway at all.
- **Warehouse street:** 12–14 m kerb to kerb with a 2.5 m concrete footway on the building side only.
- **Warehouse:** 4 storeys, floor to floor **3.6–4.0 m**, parapet at ~16 m. Loading bays at 4.5–5.5 m centres; arched opening 2.4 m wide × 3.6 m to the crown.
- **Steel shed:** eaves 7–9 m, ridge 10–11 m, bays 6 m, roller shutter 4.5 m wide × 4.5 m high.
- **Container stack:** 4 high = 10.4 m; rows 12.2 m deep with a 15–20 m running lane between blocks.
- **Ship-to-shore crane:** portal legs ~30 m apart, rail gauge 30.5 m, boom hinge ~50 m, tip with the boom raised **75–85 m** — taller than anything else in the district by a factor of six.
- **Floodlight mast:** 25–30 m, 6–12 heads, spaced 60–90 m around the yard.
- **Utility pole:** 11 m, spaced 40–50 m, lowest conductor at ~7 m over the carriageway.
- **Chain link:** 2.4 m fabric + 0.45 m barbed arms = 2.85 m total; posts at 3.0 m.
- **Storage tank:** 25–35 m diameter, 12–15 m tall, plate courses 2.4 m high with visible bolt lines; bund wall 1.5 m at 6 m clear of the shell.
- **Bollard:** 200 mm dia, 1.0–1.2 m above ground, in groups of 3–4 at 0.9 m centres.
- **Nothing over three storeys** holds true everywhere except the 19th-century warehouses (four) and the grain silo/elevator type, which is a separate landmark object.
