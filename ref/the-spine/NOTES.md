# the-spine — reference board

Anchors: Wall Street / Broadway, Lower Manhattan, New York, (40.7075, −74.0113) · Post Office Square / Liberty Square, Downtown Boston, (42.3564, −71.0553)

Coverage note: Mapillary has dense coverage of Lower Manhattan (dashcam sequences 2014–2023) and **zero coverage of downtown Boston** — repeated bbox queries out to 2 km returned an empty `data` array. The Boston half of this board is therefore all Wikimedia geosearch, which is unusually rich there (a photographer walked the whole district in March 2025 and uploaded ~50 numbered frames).

## What is actually there — observations

- **Ground:** Asphalt, and it is not one asphalt. Every photo shows at least three generations of surface in one frame — a bluish-grey mat, older lighter grey oxidised patches, and black tar snakes along the cracks and around every manhole and valve cover. Crossings are painted as **ladder/zebra bars, not two parallel lines**, and they are worn through in the wheel tracks to about 50 % coverage, so each bar is a bar with a grey stripe eaten out of its middle. Manhole covers are concentric-ring cast iron, roughly every 15–20 m, and each sits in a ring of patch asphalt about 300 mm wider than the cover, slightly proud or slightly sunk. There are **red-painted bus lanes** (a dull brick red, faded and scuffed to a mottle, not a clean colour). Pavements are grey concrete slabs, 1.5 m square, cracked and settled at manhole and tree pit, with dark chewing-gum spotting at maybe 1 per 0.5 m² near shop doors and almost none mid-block. Kerbs are granite, not concrete — grey, chipped on the outer arris, with occasional lengths painted red or yellow.
- **Facades and ground floor:** The ground floor is a *different building* from the one above it. Above: limestone ashlar, bronze spandrels, cornices, carved names. Below: black-painted panelling, a stainless canopy, backlit vinyl, blade signs. The seam is at ~4.5 m and it is abrupt. Ground-floor tenants are a sushi counter, a café, a print shop, a smoke shop, a pizza place, a chain burrito — nothing that matches the grandeur above. There is nearly always a **health-grade placard, an ATM in the door reveal, and at least one sandwich board on the pavement**. Doorways to the offices themselves are bronze and dark and give nothing away.
- **Roofline and clutter:** From the street you barely see roofs — the canyon eats them. What you do see is the **party wall of a demolished building**, whitewashed or bare brick, with the ghost of the old floor levels and chimney flues still readable, and often a billboard bolted to it. Fire escapes exist on the smaller survivor buildings. On the 3–5 storey infill you get roof-mounted mechanical boxes and a vertical caged ladder.
- **Verticality — poles, cables, signs:** No overhead wires at all. Everything is underground here — this is a real and surprising difference from every other district in this plan. The verticals are: cast-iron lamp standards (fluted, black, acorn globe on the historic streets; plain grey steel with a curved mast arm on the ordinary ones), traffic signal poles with mast arms, a dense stack of regulatory signs bolted to *one* pole rather than spread out (ONE WAY over NO STANDING over NO PARKING over a bus-route plate), banner flags on brackets at ~4 m, and a continuous run of **steel bollards** at 1.2–1.5 m spacing along the kerb.
- **Materials and their ageing:** Limestone and cast stone that has gone from cream to a pale warm grey, with black streaking under every projecting sill and cornice, running down in vertical tails 200–400 mm long. Bronze and copper spandrels oxidised to a dark chocolate-brown-going-green. Pink/salmon granite plinth courses at pavement level, polished and scuffed matte at shoe height. Brick towers of the 1920s in a dark grey-brown that is soot as much as brick. The 1980s buildings are the ones that look worst — flat aluminium panel and mirror glass with visible gasket dirt.
- **Colour:** Not grey. The dominant colours are **warm bone/cream limestone**, **chocolate-brown oxidised bronze**, **soot-black on every horizontal ledge**, **salmon-pink polished granite** at the base, and then a small number of very saturated accents that carry the whole image: the brick-red bus lane, the safety-orange-and-white barricade stripes, the yellow POLICE LINE tape, one red neon, one yellow lightbox. The sky in the canyon reads as blown-out white, not blue, because it's a narrow slot against dark walls.
- **Light:** 40.7 °N. The street floor gets direct sun only for a short window around solar noon and only on the streets that happen to run east–west; the north–south ones stay in shade all day. The rest of the time the light on a facade is **bounced light off the building opposite**, which is warm and has almost no directional shadow. That is the single most important lighting fact here. Shadow lines run as hard diagonals across the upper facades and move visibly. At night: cool white LED from the mast-arm streetlights, warm tungsten spill from lobbies, warm bulb strings under every scaffold, and lit office windows in a scattered random pattern (perhaps 1 in 8 lit at 21:00).
- **Vegetation:** Almost none, and it is all in containers. Honey locust and small-leaved lime in tree pits with steel grates, plus bay/boxwood in big precast concrete planters that double as vehicle barriers. In the March photos every tree is bare — leafless for a long season — and the branch structure is heavily pollarded/lopped where it fouled the building line. Post Office Square is the one place with real turf.
- **People and vehicles:** Very few, and clustered at specific points, not spread. At 08:30 on a weekday, Wall Street had maybe six people in frame and they were all stationary near a doorway. At a crossing you get 4–8 people in the box at once. Vehicles: black SUVs and black executive saloons parked nose-to-tail against the kerb with almost no gaps, plus one white box truck double-parked with its hazards on, plus scaffolding company vans. Very few ordinary private cars.
- **The tells:**
  - **Scaffolding everywhere, permanently.** Two-thirds of the street-level images have a sidewalk shed in them. It changes the whole silhouette of the street — it puts a 4.5 m-high flat plywood ceiling over the pavement, hides the ground floor, and turns the pavement into a tunnel.
  - Every scaffold has a strip of warm bulbs on its underside, on all day.
  - Blue-and-white health-grade placards stuck to shop glass.
  - The A-board on the pavement, always slightly in the way.
  - Steel crowd barriers left standing in the street for no visible event.
  - A run of yellow tape tied between two barriers, still there days later.
  - Ghost floor lines and flue scars on an exposed party wall.
  - A carved company name on a facade whose ground floor is now a copy shop.
  - Address plaques screwed on that contradict the carved name above them.
  - Trash bins overflowing with white bags stacked around the base.
  - Tar snakes radiating from every manhole.

**Where the photographs contradicted what I would have assumed:**
1. I would have drawn overhead traffic signals on span wires. There are none — everything is on rigid mast arms, and there is **no overhead wire of any kind** in a pre-war financial district. Save the cables for Bellcross and Kilnward, where they belong.
2. I would have made the street busy. At the coordinate, at street level, it is mostly **empty** — the crowds are a rush-hour and lunch-hour phenomenon and the default state is a wide empty carriageway.
3. I assumed the ground floor would be banks. It is takeaway food, and the tenancies are visibly cheap relative to the building.

## Numbers I could measure or infer
All estimates, read off the photographs against known objects (car ≈ 4.6 m long, 1.8 m wide; person ≈ 1.75 m; door ≈ 2.1 m; kerb ≈ 150 mm).

- **Street width, kerb to kerb:** 11–13 m on Wall Street (two 3.3 m lanes plus a 3.5 m bus lane plus edge). New Street is narrower: about 9 m kerb to kerb.
- **Pavement width:** 3.5–5 m on the main streets; under 2.5 m on the side streets, and a sidewalk shed eats about 0.4 m of it on each side with its standards.
- **Ground-floor height:** 4.5–5.5 m floor to floor. Upper office floors 3.6–3.9 m. So a 20-storey pre-war block is ≈ 80 m to the cornice.
- **Sidewalk shed clear height:** 4.3–4.6 m; decks project about 1.8 m past the kerb line.
- **Bay module on the pre-war facades:** 2.3–2.6 m centres; ashlar courses 550–650 mm.
- **Bollard spacing:** 1.2–1.5 m. **Lamp standard spacing:** 25–35 m, alternating sides.
- **Street tree spacing where they exist:** 8–10 m; tree pit 1.2 × 1.2 m.
- **Parked-car spacing at the kerb:** essentially zero gap — 0.3–0.6 m between bumpers.
- **Blade sign:** projects 0.9–1.2 m from the facade, underside at 2.8–3.2 m.
- **Canyon aspect ratio:** building height ÷ street width ranges from about 3:1 on Wall Street to well over 6:1 on New Street. That ratio is what makes the shadow, and it is the number to hold onto.
- **Block:** the grid here is not square — roughly 60–80 m on the short dimension and 150–200 m on the long, with named alleys cutting through.
