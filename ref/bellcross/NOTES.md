# bellcross — reference board

Anchors: Totterdown, Bristol, (51.4400, −2.5730) · Federal Hill / E. Cross Street, Baltimore, (39.2764, −76.6100)

Coverage note: Mapillary in Totterdown is a **single 2025 sequence along Bath Road and one 360-derived cluster at the Wells Road junction**; the famous steep painted streets (Vale Street and the streets above it) have **no Mapillary coverage at all** — bbox queries there return empty. Geograph-sourced Wikimedia photos fill that gap and are excellent. Baltimore has good 2016–2017 dashcam coverage.

This is the hero district, so the board is deliberately the biggest.

## What is actually there — observations

- **Ground:** Bristol side: black asphalt, patched but not as heavily as downtown, with a **shallow dished gutter** and a granite or concrete kerb about 100 mm proud. There are painted parking bays marked out in *thin* white lines — dashed bay ends and the word DISABLED painted into individual bays — and the paint is thin and scuffed. Pavements are 1.2–1.8 m wide, mostly grey concrete slabs or blacktop, and they are **narrower than the parked cars beside them**. Leaves drift into the gutter and against the garden walls. Baltimore side: the road surface is greyer and coarser, there are salt/grit stains in winter, the kerb is granite, and the pavement is poured concrete in ~1.2 m panels with visible saw joints. The alleys have no kerb at all on one side — the paving just runs into the wall.
- **Facades and ground floor:** Totterdown is **Bath limestone and Pennant sandstone with cast-stone dressings**, not brick — that surprised me. Two-storey terraces, each house one canted two-storey bay plus a door, with the bay carried up and roofed in its own little hipped clay-tile roof. The stone is *sometimes* painted; where it is, the colour is chosen by the owner and changes every house — pink, sky blue, cream, sage, grey, lilac, white. Where it is not, it is honey to grey-brown with heavy black streaking under sills and string courses. The Baltimore side is two- and three-storey red-orange brick with arched heads, a bracketed timber or pressed-metal cornice, and marble stoops; roughly a third of the houses have been re-clad in Formstone at some point, so the terrace alternates brick / Formstone / brick with no logic. Ground-floor shops exist only on the main road: a single-storey flat-roofed postwar shop box, or a shopfront cut into the ground floor of a rowhouse with a big picture window and a metal fascia.
- **Roofline and clutter:** Red clay double-Roman/pantile, pitched at ~35–40°, with terracotta ridge tiles and — on the better houses — a run of ridge cresting and a finial on the bay hip. **A chimney stack in every party wall**, each with four to six buff clay pots, so the roofline is a regular rhythm of stack-roof-stack. TV aerials on almost every stack; satellite dishes bolted to the *bay stonework at first-floor level*, not the roof, which is the detail I would never have got right. Velux rooflights punched into the rear slopes. Scaffolding on one house in five. Baltimore: flat or shallow roofs behind the cornice, so from the street you see the cornice and nothing else; the clutter is on the rear elevation instead — wooden decks, ladders, satellite dishes, condenser units on the flat roof.
- **Verticality — poles, cables, signs:** This is the opposite of the Spine. **Wires everywhere.** In Bristol the poles are timber, at the back of the pavement, and the drops go to hooks on the eaves of individual houses, so the wire pattern is a fan from pole to five or six houses. In Baltimore it is far heavier: multiple catenaries crossing the street diagonally at three or four different heights, with different sag on each, plus a transformer can, plus a tangle of black loops bolted to the pole where the drops are made off. Streetlights are plain grey/galvanised steel columns 5–6 m high with a small cutoff lantern (Bristol) or a cobra head on a bracket (Baltimore) — no decorative columns. Green street name plates clipped to the lighting column rather than a post of their own. Stacks of parking-regulation signs bolted to poles at every corner.
- **Materials and their ageing:** Honey Bath limestone bleaching pale and streaking black under every horizontal; grey-green Pennant rubble in the garden walls with cement-repointed patches that are visibly whiter and flatter than the original lime mortar; red clay tiles going matte and blackening on the north pitch with orange lichen on the south; render cracking and blistering at ground level where the damp rises; paint peeling first on the bay cills and the bottom of the door frames. Baltimore brick goes from orange to plum where the sun does not reach; the white timber cornice is always the freshest paint on the elevation and the most obviously repaired; Formstone weathers to a flat dead tan and its dark raked joints hold dirt.
- **Colour:** Honey and grey-brown stone; brick red-orange going plum; the clay roof is a strong burnt orange-red, not brown. The painted fronts are **pastel and desaturated** — dusty rose, powder blue, buff cream, mint, oyster grey, lilac — not primary colours; they look like exterior masonry paint that has faded two years in. Doors are the saturated notes: pillar-box red, bottle green, navy, black. Railings and gates are gloss black or bottle green. Wheelie bins are the loudest thing at ground level: local-authority green and black. Baltimore adds white marble (blue-white, dirty at the tread nosings) and the white bracketed cornice.
- **Light:** 51.4 °N in Bristol — the sun is low. In the images the shadows are long even in May, and because the terraces run across the slope, one whole side of the street is in the shade of the other for most of the day while the opposite side is fully lit. Sunlight rakes *along* the terrace and catches every bay projection, which is what makes the row read as a saw-tooth rather than a flat plane. The morning sun sits directly down the east-facing streets and blows the whole frame out. Baltimore at 39.3 °N is higher and harder. Street lighting in both is a low-mounted cutoff/cobra head, and it is **white LED now** — not the sodium orange the district's period would suggest; if you want sodium, that is a deliberate era choice, not an observation.
- **Vegetation:** Front gardens are 1.2–2.0 m deep and contain: one climbing rose, one clipped evergreen ball (box or bay), a hedge (privet or escallonia), and a bin. There are very few street trees on the terrace streets — the pavement is too narrow — so **the greenery is private, in the gardens and the back gardens, not municipal**. The park at the top has mown grass with mud worn through on the desire lines, a hawthorn/ash scrub edge, and a slide. Baltimore has street trees but they are small, in pits, and bare from November to April.
- **People and vehicles:** Almost nobody in frame. What dominates is **parked cars — continuous, both sides, nose-to-tail, at every hour**, which is the defining street-level fact of this district. Bristol: small hatchbacks and estates (Ford, VW, Nissan) plus one VW Transporter van; wing mirrors folded in because the channel is so narrow. Baltimore: sedans and a full-size pickup, and the pickup is longer than the parking bay. In both, the parked cars are the ground-plane visual texture, not the road.
- **The tells:**
  - Wheelie bins standing in the front garden all week, not just on collection day.
  - A ladder leaning against a house.
  - One house in five wrapped in scaffold with a roofer's banner on it.
  - Satellite dishes bolted into the stone of the bay at first-floor level.
  - Cast-iron star/S anchor plates in a diagonal line up an end wall.
  - A plastic waste pipe elbow discharging straight onto a rendered wall, with a stain under it.
  - The house number painted directly onto the gate pier.
  - A corrugated-plastic porch roof bolted over one door in an otherwise uniform terrace.
  - The exposed rendered party wall where a house was demolished, with the ghost of the roof pitch on it.
  - A window AC unit bolted through the brick, dripping.
  - The neighbour's Formstone stopping dead at the party line.
  - Repointing in grey cement, visibly whiter and flatter than the original.
  - Every front door a different colour, and no two houses the same paint scheme.

**Where the photographs contradicted what I would have assumed:**
1. I would have built Bellcross in **brick**. Totterdown is **stone** — honey Bath limestone bays with cast-stone dressings over a grey-green Pennant rubble base. The painted colour sits on *stone or render*, not on brick, and it reads completely differently.
2. The famous painted colour is on the **fronts only**. The rear elevations of the same houses are plain red brick with lean-to extensions and glass roofs — cheap, dark, unpainted. A district that is candy-coloured from the front and brick-brown from the back is far more interesting than one that is painted all round.
3. The bay windows have **their own little hipped tiled roofs with terracotta cresting**. That, not the paint, is what gives the terrace its silhouette.
4. There are barely any street trees. I would have lined it with them.

## Numbers I could measure or infer
Estimates, scaled off cars (4.2 m hatchback, 1.8 m wide), doors (2.0 m) and people.

- **House frontage:** 5.2–5.6 m per house in Bristol (bay + door). Baltimore rowhouse: **4.0–4.6 m** — noticeably narrower.
- **Storey height:** ground 2.7–2.9 m, first 2.5–2.7 m; eaves at ~6.0 m; ridge at ~8.5–9.0 m for a two-storey; three-storey Baltimore rowhouse cornice at ~10.5 m.
- **Bay projection:** 0.9–1.1 m from the main face, ~3.0 m wide, canted at 45°.
- **Front garden depth:** 1.2–2.0 m Bristol; **zero** in Baltimore — the stoop lands straight on the pavement.
- **Garden wall:** 0.6–0.9 m high, rubble stone, with square piers at each party line, cap ~150 mm.
- **Road width kerb to kerb:** 6.5–8.0 m. With cars parked both sides (each taking ~2.0 m from the kerb) the running channel is **2.5–4.0 m — one vehicle, and you give way**. That is the number that makes the district drive the way it should.
- **Pavement:** 1.2–1.8 m Bristol; 1.5–2.0 m Baltimore. Alley: 3.0–3.5 m wall to wall with no pavement.
- **Gradient:** the ridge steps ~600 mm every house on the steeper streets → about 11 %; the steepest anchor streets in Totterdown are around 20 %, which is at the top of what a car park brake will hold.
- **Chimney stack:** ~1.0 m wide × 0.6 m deep, 1.2–1.8 m above the ridge, 4–6 pots each 300 mm tall.
- **Pole spacing:** 30–40 m, timber, ~8 m tall Bristol; 9–11 m and much heavier in Baltimore. Wires cross at 5.5–7.0 m above the road.
- **Marble stoop:** 3 risers ≈ 0.5 m total, tread 300 mm, width 1.1 m, projecting 0.9–1.2 m into the pavement.
- **Streetlight:** 5–6 m mounting height, 30–40 m spacing, one side of the street only.
- **Parked car spacing:** nose to tail, 0.4–0.8 m gaps, continuous for the whole block with one gap at each dropped kerb.
